## Origins & History

The Arabic Parts are one of astrology's great inheritance stories. The technique itself is Hellenistic — originating in the Greek astrological tradition of the 2nd century BCE onward — where they were known simply as *Lots* (*Kleroi* in Greek). The foundational Greek texts of Dorotheus of Sidon (1st century CE), Vettius Valens (2nd century CE), and later Ptolemy all discuss the Lots as essential predictive tools.

But it was in the Islamic Golden Age — specifically the 8th to 10th centuries CE in Baghdad — that the technique was expanded, systematized, and transmitted into a form that would reshape Western astrology for the next thousand years.

The city of Baghdad had been founded in 762 CE by the Abbasid Caliph al-Mansur, who chose its location partly on astrological advice. The famous House of Wisdom (*Bayt al-Hikmah*), established under Caliph Harun al-Rashid and expanded magnificently by al-Ma'mun in the early 9th century, became the greatest intellectual institution since the Library of Alexandria. Here, translators — many of them Syriac Christians — rendered the entire accumulated knowledge of Greek, Persian, and Indian science into Arabic.

The astrological works of Ptolemy, Dorotheus, and Vettius Valens were translated, studied, and extended. Arab scholars systematized what the Greeks had sketched, expanding the original handful of Lots into over 100 calculated Arabic Parts, each addressing a specific domain of life: wealth, travel, marriage, children, illness, death, fame, and more. The Lots of Fortune, Spirit, Eros, Necessity, Courage, Victory, and Nemesis became the foundational seven — a complete cosmological vocabulary for the forces shaping a human life.

The formula is elegant in its simplicity: every Arabic Part is calculated as Ascendant + Planet A − Planet B. The resulting degree is a sensitive point in the chart, like a resonant node on a guitar string — a place where three separate cosmic frequencies combine into something new.

## The Legend

The man most responsible for transmitting this tradition to the Western world was a Persian scholar named Abu Ma'shar — known in Latin as Albumasar — who lived from 787 to 886 CE and became arguably the most influential astrologer of the entire medieval period.

But Abu Ma'shar did not begin as an astrologer. He began as a scholar of Hadith — the recorded sayings of the Prophet Muhammad — and he reportedly despised astrology, considering it incompatible with Islamic scholarship. Then, in what must rank as one of history's most consequential academic rivalries, the philosopher al-Kindī intervened.

Al-Kindī — himself a towering figure of the Islamic Golden Age, known as "the Philosopher of the Arabs" — recognized that Abu Ma'shar was a formidable intellect being wasted on memorization. He reportedly provoked a public dispute with Abu Ma'shar, humiliating him in debate, and then suggested — perhaps with calculated subtlety — that Abu Ma'shar would benefit from studying mathematics. The implicit insult was clear: you cannot argue with me until you understand numbers.

Abu Ma'shar, already in his late forties, picked up the challenge. He began studying mathematics. Through mathematics, he discovered geometry. Through geometry, he discovered the beauty of astronomical calculation. Through astronomy, he came to understand that the cosmic order of the heavens was not superstition but a vast, lawful, measurable system that encoded meaningful patterns in the positions of the planets.

He never looked back. Abu Ma'shar spent the rest of his long life — he lived to ninety-nine — producing a body of work that transformed astrology from a scattered collection of techniques into a coherent philosophical discipline. His *Kitāb al-mudkhal al-kabīr* ("Great Introduction to Astrology") was translated into Latin by John of Seville in 1133 CE and became the standard astrological textbook in European universities for over a century. His influence reached Roger Bacon, Albertus Magnus, and eventually into the intellectual tradition that produced both Copernicus and Newton.

Through Abu Ma'shar's work, the Arabic Parts — those invisible calculated points, that geometry of converging forces — entered the bloodstream of European thought. The Lot of Fortune in particular became a cornerstone of medieval astrological practice: the house containing it was considered the zone of life most blessed by fortune's attention.

## Core Philosophy

The Arabic Parts are built on a radical insight: that fate is not a single thread but a weave. No single planet, no single house, tells the full story. Fortune arises at the intersection — where the Ascendant (the body and its circumstances), the Moon (the soul and its fluctuations), and the Sun (the will and its direction) converge to a single degree. That convergence point is not fixed in the sky. It is unique to you — calculated from the specific geometry of your birth moment — and it points to the area of life where these three fundamental forces of selfhood have agreed to meet.

The seven classic Lots represent seven essential questions: Where does fortune gather? Where does spirit direct itself? What draws the heart with desire? What feels necessary and inescapable? What will you find the courage to face? Where will victory become possible? And what unavoidable reckoning awaits?
