# Biorhythm: The Science-Adjacent Art of Reading Your Inner Cycles

*By the Mythsensus Team*

Your body has never been fooled by the calendar. While your schedule tells you Monday is a workday like any other, your cells have been running their own programs since the day you were born — cycling through peaks and valleys on rhythms that answer to biology, not to the clock on the wall. Biorhythm theory is the modern attempt to chart these cycles. It sits at the peculiar, fascinating borderland between science and mysticism: too personal to be pure science, too structured to be pure intuition.

## Origins & History

Biorhythm theory emerged not from a single visionary mind but from three independent researchers working in the scientific capitals of Europe in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

The first was Wilhelm Fliess (1858–1928), a Berlin physician and one of the most intellectually unusual figures in the history of medicine. Fliess was Sigmund Freud's closest friend during the period when Freud was developing psychoanalysis — their decade-long correspondence, published as *The Complete Letters of Sigmund Freud to Wilhelm Fliess*, is one of the most intimate documents in the history of ideas. Fliess was also, to put it plainly, a man of extreme and idiosyncratic theories. He believed the nose was the nexus of all human health and performed nasal surgeries on some of Freud's patients (with disastrous results). But woven through his eccentricities was a genuine observation: studying his patients over years, he noticed that certain physical symptoms — fevers, discharges, periods of vitality — seemed to recur on a 23-day cycle in men and a 28-day cycle in women. He called these the "male" and "female" periodic rhythms. Published in 1906 as *The Course of Life: Foundations of Exact Biology*, his ideas attracted both serious attention and fierce skepticism.

Simultaneously and independently, Hermann Swoboda (1873–1963), a Viennese psychologist, arrived at exactly the same cycles while studying his own patients' emotional patterns. He published his work in 1904, leading to a bitter priority dispute with Fliess that became one of the minor academic scandals of Viennese intellectual life.

The third researcher, Friedrich Teltscher (1885–1941), was an engineer and mathematics teacher in Innsbruck. Analyzing the academic performance patterns of his students, he identified a recurring 33-day cycle that seemed to govern intellectual performance — when students were at their sharpest, when they were dull, and when they were most likely to make errors. He added this intellectual cycle to the existing framework, completing the classic three-cycle biorhythm model.

The theory gained mainstream cultural attention in the 1970s, when the combination of personal computers (which could easily calculate and chart the sinusoidal curves) and the human potential movement made biorhythm charting accessible and fashionable. Japan Airlines (JAL) incorporated biorhythm analysis into crew scheduling during the 1970s and 1980s, attempting to avoid scheduling pilots and crew on days identified as "critical" — when one or more cycles crossed the zero line. Several major corporations, professional sports teams, and even military organizations experimented with the system. Muhammad Ali's boxing matches were reportedly analyzed through the lens of biorhythms by fans and commentators of the era.

## The Legend

There is no mythology in the conventional sense here — no gods, no cosmic serpents. The "legend" of biorhythm is a human story: the story of pattern-seekers in the age of science.

Fliess was, at his core, a man haunted by a beautiful idea — that beneath the chaos of human health and behavior, there were rhythms as regular as the tides. He spent decades measuring, counting, calculating. His methods were not always rigorous by later standards; his claims were not always modest. And yet the observation at the core of his work — that the human body is not a machine running at constant capacity but a cycling system with regular periods of strength and weakness — is not wrong. It just describes something more complex than any three sine curves can capture.

The chronobiology research of the late 20th century confirmed, in far more sophisticated terms, what Fliess had groped toward: the body has circadian rhythms (24-hour cycles governing alertness, hormone levels, immune function), ultradian rhythms (cycles shorter than a day), and infradian rhythms (cycles longer than a day, including the menstrual cycle, seasonal mood patterns, and immune oscillations). These are not metaphysical claims — they are observable, measurable biological facts. Biorhythm theory is not validated by modern science in its specific claims (the 23-28-33 day cycles, starting from birth, have not held up to rigorous double-blind testing). But the underlying intuition — that your body moves in waves, that some days are genuinely better than others for physical exertion, emotional openness, or mental focus — sits in the same neighborhood as confirmed science. Just imprecisely drawn.

## Core Philosophy

Biorhythm operates from a strikingly democratic premise: every person's cycle runs from the same starting point (birth) and follows the same mathematical pattern. There is no special destiny, no inherited advantage. Your physical cycle peaked the same number of days after birth as every other person born on your birthday. What varies is the current phase — where you are in the wave today, which cycles are aligned and which are in tension. The wisdom of biorhythm, at its most practical, is permission: permission to honor the days when everything flows easily, and to be gentler with yourself on the days when the wave is in a trough.
