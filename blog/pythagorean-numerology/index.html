Pythagoras was born around 570 BCE on the island of Samos, off the coast of what is now Turkey. Even by the extraordinary standards of the ancient world, his biography reads like myth. He traveled â€” extensively, perhaps obsessively â€” before founding his school. He studied under Egyptian priests in the temples of Memphis and Thebes, where he was reportedly initiated into the deepest mysteries of sacred geometry. He journeyed to Babylon, where the Chaldean astronomer-priests shared their knowledge of celestial cycles and sacred numbers. Some accounts suggest he traveled as far as India, absorbing the mathematical and spiritual traditions of the subcontinent.

When Pythagoras finally settled â€” around 530 BCE in Croton, a Greek colony in southern Italy â€” he founded not a school in the modern sense but a secret brotherhood. The Pythagorean Society was part philosophical academy, part religious community, part conspiracy. Members followed strict rules: vegetarianism, communal living, ritual silence, and absolute secrecy. Mathematical discoveries made within the brotherhood were not to be shared outside.

The brotherhood's core belief was startling in its scope: all is number. Not metaphorically â€” literally. Numbers were the fundamental substance of reality. The monad (1) was the source of all existence. The dyad (2) represented division and polarity. The triad (3) was harmony. The tetrad (4) was justice, completeness, the physical world. Together, the first four numbers summed to ten â€” the tetractys â€” which Pythagoreans considered so sacred they swore oaths upon it.

This philosophy created the world's first number-mysticism: a coherent system in which every number from 1 to 9 carried distinct cosmic qualities, and the numerical values of names and birth dates revealed truths about the soul inhabiting a body. This is the direct ancestor of the numerological tradition we use today.

The Legend

The brotherhood was dangerous to betray.

Hippasus of Metapontum was a Pythagorean initiate â€” a true believer in the sacred harmony of number. But he made a discovery that threatened to unravel everything the brotherhood held sacred. He proved, through geometric reasoning, that the square root of 2 is an irrational number â€” a number that cannot be expressed as any ratio of two whole numbers. It goes on forever: 1.41421356... without end, without pattern, without resolution.

For the Pythagoreans, this was heresy. If all is number, and numbers were understood as ratios between whole numbers, then irrational numbers were an impossibility â€” a crack in the mathematical foundation of the cosmos. Hippasus had found a flaw in God's arithmetic. He had looked into the abyss between numbers and found that it was infinite.

According to legend, he was drowned at sea by his fellow Pythagoreans â€” thrown from a ship for the crime of revealing the existence of the irrational. Whether this story is historical or symbolic, it captures something true about the Pythagorean worldview: this was not casual philosophy. Numbers were sacred. Their secrets were dangerous. And the man who broke the code of silence paid with his life.

Pythagoras himself died around 495 BCE, during a period of violent persecution of his brotherhood. But the ideas survived. Plato absorbed them. Neoplatonist philosophers carried them forward.
