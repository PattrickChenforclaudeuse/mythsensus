The Legend

Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai lived in Roman-occupied Judea in the 2nd century CE. He had studied under Rabbi Akiva, the great sage who was eventually martyred by the Romans. Shimon bar Yochai himself earned Roman attention â€” according to the Talmud, he made a public remark critical of Roman rule. Informed that the Romans intended to execute him, he fled with his son Eleazar.

They hid in a cave. According to legend, a carob tree and a spring of water miraculously appeared at the cave entrance to sustain them. For twelve years, they remained in the cave, and during those years, Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai received the entirety of Kabbalistic wisdom through divine illumination. The two of them sat buried in sand up to their necks to preserve their clothing, studying and meditating, their minds turning entirely toward the divine.

When they finally emerged after twelve years, the legend says that Shimon's spiritual perception had become so intensely refined that wherever he looked with anger or judgment, fire would spring up and consume what he saw. The ordinary world â€” with its compromise and imperfection â€” was intolerable to a consciousness that had spent twelve years in direct contact with divine light. Farmers tending fields, merchants conducting trade: to Rabbi Shimon's purified eyes, they seemed to be abandoning eternal truth for trivial concerns. Where he looked with disapproval, the things burned.

He and his son returned to the cave for another year. When they emerged again, his gaze had softened â€” "what my son destroys, I can repair," the tradition says of this second emergence. He had learned to hold both truths at once: the perfection of the divine and the imperfection of the human world, and the necessity of moving between them.

God descends to meet the human. The mystic ascends to meet the divine. The soul, in Kabbalistic understanding, is a spark of divine light that has chosen the experience of limitation. The point of the journey is the return â€” and the tools of return are the 22 paths, the 10 Sefirot, and the letters that built the world.
